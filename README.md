# Best coding interview prep: how to pick by interview window and budget, plus what structured courses actually cost

Most people asking about the best coding interview prep are really asking one of two questions. Either "which platform will make me stop wasting months," or "is this specific paid course worth the money." Those are different problems, and almost every comparison article answers them badly by ranking eight platforms in a listicle and calling LeetCode the winner.

So here's a more useful approach: decide based on two facts about your situation — which interview round you're actually being tested on, and how many weeks you have. Everything else, including price, is a secondary filter.

## The two variables that decide everything

**Your round type.** A 45-minute algorithm screen, a take-home, a system design round, and a behavioral conversation test completely different skills. Spending $159 on company-tagged problems does nothing for a system design round. Reading a system design course does nothing for a timed screen where you need to recognize that a problem is sliding window before the clock runs out.

**Your window.** With six weeks, a 3,000-problem bank is a liability — there is no defined endpoint and no way to know whether you're done. With six months, a compact curriculum that takes four to eight weeks can feel thin once you've finished it, and you'll want volume practice on top.

That's the whole decision. Nearly every platform is good at one of these and mediocre at the others.

## Matching prep tools to the round you're facing

| Interview round | What actually gets tested | Tools people commonly pair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timed algorithm screen (phone screen / OA) | Fast pattern recognition under pressure | A pattern curriculum + a problem bank |
| Company-specific onsite coding | Known-topic recall, clean implementation | Pattern curriculum + company-tagged practice |
| System design (mid to senior) | Tradeoff reasoning, structure | A dedicated system design course |
| Behavioral | Narrative structure, STAR answers | Coaching, peer mocks |
| Live coding with communication | Thinking out loud, handling follow-ups | Peer or AI mock interviews |

The honest implication is that most candidates end up running two or three tools at once. That's normal and not a sign you bought the wrong thing. What matters is that the pattern-recognition piece — the part that determines whether you spend the first 15 of your 45 minutes guessing at approaches — is covered solidly before you start grinding volume.

## Where AlgoMonster stops being the answer

Being specific about the gaps matters more than listing strengths.

**Problem volume for top-tier loops.** Around 230 problems teaches the frameworks. It doesn't build the raw speed that comes from a few hundred reps. Most candidates pairing it with LeetCode treat AlgoMonster as the teacher and LeetCode as the gym, which is the workflow reviews consistently describe.

**System design depth.** There's introductory system design content — load balancing, caching, sharding-level material. For L5-and-above loops where system design is its own full round, that isn't sufficient, and you'll need a dedicated resource.

**Behavioral prep.** Same story. There's some coverage, and the platform wasn't designed around it. Amazon's Leadership Principles, Google's Googleyness, and Meta's impact questions need their own preparation.

**No live interview simulation.** Every problem is solved at your own pace with no timer and no one asking follow-ups. Pattern knowledge and performance under observation are different skills, and AlgoMonster only builds the first. Add peer mocks or an AI mock interviewer before your onsite.

**Text only, no video.** If you learn by watching someone solve a problem on screen, this format will feel like a slog regardless of how good the content is. That's a real reason to pick NeetCode or AlgoExpert instead, not a knock on the curriculum.

## A six-week plan that uses a pattern course properly

The curriculum is designed to be completed in four to eight weeks at one to two hours a day. Rushing it in two weeks tends to produce surface-level recognition without the underlying decision logic, which is exactly what you need on the day.

1. **Weeks 1–4:** Work the pattern curriculum in order. Don't skip the flowcharts. When a problem looks familiar, cover the solution and identify the pattern out loud before you write anything.
2. **Weeks 3–5 (overlapping):** Start adding volume practice from a separate problem bank on the patterns you've finished. The point is speed, not new concepts.
3. **Week 5:** Begin live practice — peer mocks or AI mock interviews. Speak your reasoning. Get comfortable being asked "why not the other approach?"
4. **Week 6:** Use Speedrun to cycle back through every pattern in compressed form. Book one or two mocks in the final days, and spend whatever's left on the round that scares you most, whether that's system design or behavioral.

If your interview is two weeks out, this plan doesn't apply. At that distance a pattern review plus targeted mocks beats trying to absorb a new curriculum, and the free tier is probably enough.
